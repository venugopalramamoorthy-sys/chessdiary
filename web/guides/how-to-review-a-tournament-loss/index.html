HOW TO REVIEW A TOURNAMENT LOSS: A STEP-BY-STEP METHOD

By Venugopal Ramamoorthy — tournament chess player and creator of ChessDiary

Most players do one of two things after a loss. They replay it in their head for hours, usually the same few moves, or they never look at it again. Neither produces improvement. The game contains information, but only a structured review extracts it.

The method below takes about thirty minutes per game. It works with a paper scoresheet, a PGN or an app. The point is not to find the engine's best move at every turn; it is to find why you missed what you missed.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  • Write down what you were thinking before you open an engine; the engine cannot tell you what you saw.
  • Look at the two or three biggest evaluation swings, not every flagged move.
  • End every review with one concrete rule, then count your mistake categories every month.

STEP 1: WRITE DOWN WHAT YOU THOUGHT, BEFORE THE ENGINE

As soon as you can after the game, ideally within an hour, write a few lines without looking at any analysis. Include:

  • the moment you thought the game turned, in your opinion;
  • where you spent the most time, and what you were trying to work out;
  • what you were worried about and what you were hoping for;
  • how you felt: confident, tired, rushed, annoyed.

This is the step that is most often skipped, and it is the most valuable. Once you see the engine's verdict, your memory rewrites itself: you start to believe you always knew the right plan. Your own notes are the only record of what you actually saw at the board.

STEP 2: GET THE GAME INTO ONE PLACE

You need the full move list in a form you can replay. If you played over the board, that means turning the scoresheet into a digital game. See How to Read (and Keep) a Chess Scoresheet for common notation pitfalls, and How to Photograph a Scoresheet for Accurate OCR if you plan to scan it. Whatever method you use, replay the game once to confirm that the moves are right before you analyse anything. A single misread move produces a nonsense review.

STEP 3: REPLAY WITH YOUR NOTES, THEN RUN THE ENGINE

Go through the game once, move by move, with your notes beside you. Mark the places where you were surprised, where you thought for a long time, and where your notes say the game turned. Then run the engine.

Do not read every flagged move. Look at the evaluation graph and find the two or three biggest swings, and the point where the evaluation first moved against you. Those are the moves worth understanding. A 30-centipawn inaccuracy in move 14 is almost never the lesson; a 250-centipawn swing in move 23 usually is. For more on judging swing size, see Centipawns and Win Probability.

STEP 4: ASK THREE QUESTIONS ABOUT EACH SWING

  1. What did I see? What move and what line did I consider at the board, and what did I decide it led to?
  2. What did I miss? What does the engine show that I did not consider: an opponent's reply, a tactic, a better plan?
  3. Why did I miss it? This is the useful question. Pick a cause, not just a description.

Be honest about the answer to the third question. "I blundered" is not a cause. "I did not check for knight forks after the recapture" is.

STEP 5: CLASSIFY THE CAUSE

Over time, the categories you pick will cluster. That clustering is the entire point of keeping a record. A workable set of categories:

CategoryWhat it looks likeA typical fix
Tactical oversightA fork, pin, back-rank mate or hanging piece you did not noticeA short pre-move scan for the opponent's checks, captures and threats
Calculation errorYou saw the idea but miscounted or stopped too earlyWrite out lines, check the opponent's best reply at every step
Positional misjudgmentYou made a plan that did not suit the structureName the pawn structure before choosing a plan
Opening knowledgeYou were out of book and misplayed a known positionStudy the specific line, not the whole opening
Time troubleThe mistake came after you were already short of timeReview where the time went earlier in the game
Endgame techniqueA won or drawn ending went wrongStudy the exact ending type
MindsetYou played too fast, too passively or too riskily because of how you feltNote the pattern and plan a routine for it

STEP 6: WRITE ONE RULE

End every review with a single sentence, written as an instruction to yourself that you can apply in the next game. Good rules are concrete and checkable:

  • "Before any rook trade, check whether my king has a flight square."
  • "After every capture, list the squares an enemy knight can reach."
  • "If I have used more than ten minutes on a move, play the safest candidate."

A vague lesson like "be more careful" gives you nothing to do. A rule gives you one action at one moment of the game.

STEP 7: COUNT, EVERY MONTH

One review teaches you about one game. A month of reviews teaches you about yourself. Once a month, go through your reviews and count the categories. The most frequent cause is your actual weakness, and it is very often not the one you would have guessed. If twelve of your last twenty losses include a tactical oversight on the move after a capture, that is where to spend your training time.

COMMON MISTAKES IN REVIEWING

  • Following the engine's best move without understanding it. If the engine's line sacrifices a piece for a position you would never find over the board, the lesson is not to find it. The lesson is the simpler move you could have played.
  • Reviewing only losses. Review wins too, especially wins where you were lucky. They show what your opponents get wrong and what you also got wrong.
  • Spending too long. Thirty minutes of focused review beats three hours of drifting through lines. If a game needs more, make a note and come back.
  • Blaming the opening. A bad opening result is usually a bad middlegame decision a few moves later.

None of this requires a coach or special software. It requires a place to put the game, a place to put your notes, and the discipline to write the one rule. Everything else is a refinement.
